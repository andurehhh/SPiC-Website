Ethics and Morality

Foundations, Perspectives, and Contemporary Applications in Computing

Defining the Ethical Landscape

Ethics originated from the Greek word "ethos", meaning "way of living."

It is a branch of philosophy dedicated to the study of human behavior, particularly in social situations. It serves as an external framework designed to guide behavior, uphold public trust, and help individuals lead virtuous lives by making informed choices between good and evil.

The Baumhart Study (1960s)

Sociologist and Jesuit priest Rev. Raymond Baumhart found that most people equate ethics with subjective markers rather than universal principles. However, he argued that ethics is NOT about:

Feelings

Gut feelings can provide answers but often stop us from behaving ethically when fears or interests conflict. Feelings are subjective, inconsistent, and can be biased.

Religious Beliefs

Religion offers moral guidance for believers but does not provide a comprehensive basis for right and wrong in a diverse, global society.

Historical Foundations

Ancient literary works—the Epic of Gilgamesh, Homer's Iliad, and the Icelandic Eddas—insightfully highlight courage and justice as the paramount traits of leadership. They suggest that actions against malevolent entities or deviant behavior can be morally justified.

Socrates

Believed happiness is evident in terms of a long-term effect on the soul. He taught that no one knowingly does wrong; people only act badly due to ignorance.

Plato

Argued for a soul in harmony. Morality is the result of reason ruling over spirit and appetite. True morality stems from understanding the "Form of the Good."

Aristotle

The ultimate goal is Eudaimonia (human flourishing). Virtues are character traits that enable us to function excellently as human beings.

Eastern Ethical Systems

Dharma & Karma

Dharma refers to the cosmic order or universal law. On an individual level, it is the righteous duty one must follow to live in harmony with the universe.

  • Svadharma: Personal role in life (e.g., student, parent).
  • Sanatana Dharma: Eternal duties like non-violence (ahimsa) and compassion.

Karma is the principle of cause and effect; acting in accordance with dharma leads to spiritual liberation (Moksha).

Buddhism & Confucianism

The Noble Eightfold Path provides a framework for ethical conduct through Right Speech, Action, and Livelihood.

Confucius emphasized self-cultivation through the Five Constants: Ren (benevolence), Li (propriety), Yi (righteousness), Zhi (wisdom), and Xin (sincerity).

Major Western Frameworks

Natural Law Theory

St. Thomas Aquinas

Universal moral principles are inherent in human nature and discoverable by reason. He proposed a hierarchy: Eternal Law, Divine Law, Natural Law, and Human Law.

Kantian Deontology

Immanuel Kant

Moral decisions must be based on Duty. He posited the Categorical Imperative—a universal and unconditional moral law that applies to all rational beings regardless of outcome.

Utilitarianism

Jeremy Bentham & J.S. Mill

Morally correct behaviors are those that yield the highest level of overall happiness for the largest possible group. It is often summarized as "the end justifies the means."

The 4 Pillars of Ethical Practice

These foundational principles serve as a framework for analyzing complex moral dilemmas in professional computing and social environments.

01. Autonomy

This principle emphasizes the individual's right to self-determination. In professional practice, it involves respecting a person's capacity to make informed decisions without coercion or manipulation.

It requires absolute transparency and the provision of all necessary information, ensuring that consent is always valid and voluntary.

02. Beneficence

Beneficence is the active obligation to do good. It goes beyond simply avoiding harm; it requires proactive steps to contribute to the welfare of others and promote the general well-being of the community.

Professionals must actively seek ways for their systems and policies to provide positive value to society.

03. Nonmaleficence

Often considered the most foundational concept, this dictates the duty to "do no harm." It involves avoiding the infliction of unnecessary pain, suffering, or risk of loss.

This requires a rigorous evaluation of potential negative impacts before implementing new technologies or computing systems.

04. Justice

Justice is centered on fairness and equity. It ensures that the benefits and burdens of a system are distributed fairly among all individuals, mandating equal treatment and access.

In computing, this means ensuring that technology does not discriminate based on social status, background, or identity.

Morality Frameworks

While Ethics are agreed rules for a group, Morality is a personal code formed by upbringing and life experiences. It is often the "gut feeling" tied to one's identity.

Moral Absolutism

Certain actions are inherently right or wrong regardless of the context, consequences, or intentions (e.g., lying is always wrong even to save a life).

Moral Relativism

Judgments are relative to cultural context or historical periods. No single universal moral code exists; different systems are equally valid.
